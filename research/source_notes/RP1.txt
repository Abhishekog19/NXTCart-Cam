Abstract—To address low checkout efficiency and manual
errors in traditional supermarkets, this paper proposes an
intelligent shopping cart product recognition system that fuses
computer vision with multi-sensor information. The system is built
around an embedded edge terminal integrating an RGB-D camera,

multimodal fusion decision model that combines visual confidence
scores, measured weight and RFID tag IDs. The framework
reduces ambiguity in single-modality recognition, particularly
under occlusion, similar-looking packaging and tag occlusion.
Experiments are conducted on a dataset containing 1000 common

meeting real-time checkout requirements on embedded hardware.
The system supports automatic product recognition, smart billing
and shopping-list generation, effectively shortening checkout time
and reducing operating costs, and can serve as a practical
reference for intelligent retail upgrades.

I. I NTRODUCTION
In the past few years, digital transformation has changed
how people buy groceries and everyday goods. Large and small
retailers are experimenting with self-checkout lanes, mobile
payment and AI-assisted services to reduce queues and improve
in-store experience [1]. Global surveys on self-checkout
indicate that retailers adopt these systems to cut labor costs and
increase throughput, but they also face challenges such as
shrinkage, user errors and system downtime [2]. At the same
time, customers have become more tolerant of technology in the
store, yet they still expect the checkout process to be fast,
predictable and transparent.
Radio-frequency identification (RFID) is one of the most
widely discussed technologies for automatic checkout. Industry
reports show that retailers increasingly tag items with RFID to
support real-time inventory, loss prevention and rapid stock
counting [3]. In supermarket and department-store settings,
RFID-based self-checkout systems allow customers to place
tagged products into a read zone and complete payment without
scanning each barcode [4]. Recent reviews of RFID-enabled
shopping carts summarize their potential benefits but also point
out practical issues, including tag cost, read collisions and
interference from liquid or metallic packaging [5]. In practice,
tag failures, misreads and missing tags limit the reliability of an
RFID-only solution, especially in crowded carts.
In parallel, deep learning has made computer vision a strong
candidate for product recognition in retail environments.
Pietrini et al. developed a deep learning-based shelf monitoring
system to automatically verify on-shelf availability and
planogram compliance, showing that convolutional networks
can handle cluttered shelves with many small items [6]. Depth
cameras and RGB-D setups have also been explored for on-
shelf availability estimation and anomaly detection, for
example using depth cues to distinguish fronts from background
[7]. Sun et al. proposed a deep learning-based product
recognition system for intelligent retail environments, which
integrates visual detection with store-specific metadata [8]. On
the data side, large-scale multi-modal e-commerce datasets
such as MEP-3M and its automatic checkout subset provide
millions of product images and attributes, enabling more robust
representation learning for retail scenarios [9].
Automatic checkout is increasingly treated as a multimodal
problem rather than a purely visual one. Recent work on edge-
embedded multi-feature fusion networks for automatic
checkout combines visual features with auxiliary signals such
as shelf location or historical transaction patterns to improve
robustness on resource-constrained devices [10]. Transformer-
based multimodal fusion models have been proposed to jointly
encode product images, textual attributes and structural

combining complementary signals can help resolve ambiguous

monitoring or on fixed checkout counters, rather than on mobile
shopping carts.
Object detection itself has evolved rapidly. YOLOv8
provides an efficient, one-stage detection family with improved
backbone, feature fusion and anchor-free heads, achieving a
good balance between speed and accuracy for many domains
[12]. Variants such as DC-YOLOv8 further optimize the
architecture for detecting small objects by modifying the feature
pyramid and attention mechanisms [13]. Other work explores
transfer learning and fine-tuning strategies to adapt YOLOv8 to
specific application domains, showing that even relatively small
models can reach strong performance when trained carefully on
domain data [14]. For embedded devices like an intelligent cart,
these compact detectors are attractive because they can still run
in real time while leaving resources for other tasks, such as
sensor fusion and user interface.
Smart shopping carts aim to bring these technologies closer
to the customer. Liang et al. presented a full self-service smart
shopping cart that integrates sensors, displays and networking
to support autonomous checkout and shopping assistance [15].
Earlier prototypes and concept systems based on RFID-enabled
carts also illustrate how carts can track items and communicate
with back-end systems. However, many of these designs rely on
a single dominant modality—often RFID or barcodes—with
other sensors used only as auxiliary checks. When the main
modality fails because of occlusion, tag problems or confusing
packaging, the system typically falls back to manual
intervention, which reduces the perceived value of automation
and can be frustrating for users. Findings from self-checkout
studies also suggest that inconsistent behavior and frequent
exceptions are major sources of user dissatisfaction [2].
Given this context, there is a need for shopping cart systems
that combine computer vision, RFID and physical sensing in
a more balanced way. A cart-mounted RGB-D camera can
capture rich visual information about products placed into the
basket, while a weight sensor provides a simple physical
constraint based on the change in mass. RFID tags, when
available, supply a unique identifier that ties the product to the
store’s inventory system [3], [4], [15]. By fusing these three
modalities on an embedded processor using a modern object
detector such as YOLOv8 [11]–[13], it should be possible to
reach higher recognition accuracy than any single modality can
provide alone, while still meeting real-time requirements and
respecting store cost constraints.
In this paper, we propose an intelligent shopping cart
product recognition system that follows this multimodal design
philosophy. The cart is instrumented with an RGB-D camera, a
weight sensor and an RFID reader, all connected to an
embedded edge processor. On top of this hardware, we adopt a
YOLOv8-based detector as the visual backbone [11]–[13], and
we design a decision-level fusion module that combines visual
confidence scores with weight-based candidates and RFID-
based identity cues, inspired by recent multimodal and edge-
centric automatic checkout frameworks [8]–[10]. The overall
system is evaluated in a realistic supermarket-like setting with
1000 common products, under varying viewpoints, lighting and
occlusion. Our results show that the proposed fusion design can
significantly improve recognition accuracy and reduce
misidentification, compared to vision-only and RFID-only
baselines, while keeping the average per-item latency around
0.3 s.
The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section
II details the hardware architecture, data acquisition pipeline
and multimodal fusion methodology. Section III presents the
dataset, experimental setup and quantitative results. Section IV
discusses design trade-offs, limitations and deployment
considerations in the context of current smart retail practices.
Section V concludes the paper and outlines future research
directions in multimodal product recognition and intelligent
cart design.

III. E XPERIMENTS
A. Dataset and Collection Protocol
We collected a dataset of 1000 supermarket products drawn
from typical grocery categories: beverages, snacks, dry goods,
fresh produce in pre-packed trays, personal care items and
household goods. For each SKU, we captured RGB-D images
in a cart-like setting under:
Three lighting conditions (bright, normal, slightly dim),
Three camera heights and angles, and
Multiple degrees of occlusion (no occlusion, partial
occlusion, strong occlusion).
Each capture sequence includes the moment when the
product is placed into the basket, handled by a human subject in
natural motion. In total, the dataset contains approximately
75,000 labeled frames and 80,000 event instances (product
additions and removals). For each event, we store the image
crop, weight change and RFID tag ID corresponding to the
product, along with ground truth SKU labels.
The dataset is split into 70% training, 10% validation and

B. Baseline Methods and Implementation Details
We compare the proposed system to two baselines:
Vision-only YOLOv8. This baseline uses the same
YOLOv8-s detector as our system but treats the highest-
confidence detection per event as the final decision without
weight or RFID information.
RFID-only recognition. This baseline assumes that each
SKU has a unique RFID tag ID and uses tag reads to infer which
product has been added or removed. If multiple new tags appear
simultaneously, a simple heuristic is used based on historical
co-occurrence patterns.
All methods run on the same embedded hardware platform.
We measure:
Top-1 recognition accuracy at the event level,
Misidentification rate (fraction of events with incorrect
SKU),
Average per-event latency (from weight change to final
prediction), and
False-positive event rate, where the system reports a
spurious addition or removal.
YOLOv8-s is trained for 200 epochs with cosine learning
rate scheduling, using pre-trained COCO weights as
initialization. The loss combines bounding box regression,
objectness and classification components. For the fusion model,
the coefficientsߙ,ߚ ,ߛ and decision thresholds are tuned on the
validation set to maximize accuracy under a latency constraint.

E. Robustness Under Occlusion and Similar Packaging
We further analyze performance under specific challenging
conditions:
Heavy occlusion, where more than 40% of the product area
is occluded by other items or the cart wall;
Visually similar items, such as different flavors of the same
brand of beverage in identical bottles; and
Tag conflict, where multiple new RFID tags are detected
simultaneously.
On heavily occluded events, vision-only accuracy drops to
86.2%, while the fusion model maintains 95.6% accuracy by
leveraging weight and tag information. For visually similar
items with identical bottle shapes, the fusion model resolves a
large fraction of confusion by relying on distinct weights and
tag IDs. In tag-conflict scenarios, the fusion model falls back to
visual confidence and weight consistency to select the correct
SKU.
IV. C ONCLUSION
This paper presented an intelligent shopping cart product
recognition system that fuses computer vision and multi-sensor
information to deliver accurate, real-time checkout in
supermarket environments. Starting from the practical pain
points of traditional cashier lanes—long queues, manual
scanning errors and limited visibility into cart contents—we
designed a cart-mounted embedded platform that combines an
RGB-D camera, a weight sensor and an RFID reader under a
unified decision framework. On top of this hardware, we built a
perception stack centered on YOLOv8-s for product detection,
complemented by weight-based candidate filtering and RFID-
based identity cues. The core of the solution is a multimodal
fusion module that integrates the strengths of each modality
while providing clear handling of conflicts and uncertainty.
Using a dataset of 1000 common supermarket products
captured under realistic cart conditions, we evaluated the fusion
system against a vision-only YOLOv8 baseline and an RFID-
only baseline. The proposed system achieved 98.7% event-level
recognition accuracy with a misidentification rate of 0.8% and
an average per-item latency of 0.3 s on an embedded SoC.
These results demonstrate that the combination of RGB-D
vision, weight sensing and RFID can surpass single-modality
systems in both accuracy and robustness while meeting real-
time constraints. Ablation studies confirmed that each modality
contributes meaningfully to performance, and that the full
fusion configuration offers the best trade-off between reliability
and computational cost.
